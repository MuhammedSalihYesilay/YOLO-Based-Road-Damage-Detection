## LITERATURE ANALYSIS

### 1. Road Damage Datasets and Challenges

Progress in automatic road damage detection has largely followed the release of public datasets and the open competitions built on them. One of the first large collections came from Maeda et al. [1]: working with seven Japanese municipalities, they mounted a smartphone on a car and recorded 9,053 images containing 15,435 damage instances. Using a detector based on a convolutional neural network (CNN), they were able to separate the damage into eight types with high accuracy [1]. The 2018 IEEE Big Data Cup then hosted the first Road Damage Detection Challenge, RDDC'2018, which was limited to Japan [5]. Fifty-nine teams took part, and the winning entry, an ensemble of Faster R-CNN and SSD detectors, scored 0.665 [5].

RDD2020 [2] widened the scope to three countries. Its 26,336 images from India, Japan, and the Czech Republic contain over 31,000 damage instances, recorded with smartphones mounted on vehicles and labeled in the PASCAL VOC format [2]. The label set was also narrowed to four classes: longitudinal cracks (D00), transverse cracks (D10), alligator cracks (D20), and potholes (D40) [2]. This dataset was used in the Global Road Damage Detection Challenge (GRDDC'2020), another IEEE Big Data Cup, for which 121 teams registered [3]. The top solution combined several YOLO models in an ensemble and reached F1 scores of 0.67 and 0.66 on the two test sets [3].

RDD2022 [4] is the latest dataset in this line. With 47,420 images from Japan, India, the Czech Republic, Norway, the United States, and China, it holds more than 55,000 damage instances and keeps the same four damage types [4]. It was published together with the Crowdsensing-based Road Damage Detection Challenge (CRDDC'2022) [4]. In this challenge, the choice of model architecture was left open to the participants [5]. The images come from several capture setups, among them vehicle-mounted smartphones, wide-angle cameras, drones, and motorbikes, and they differ in resolution [5]; the organizers pointed out that the high-resolution part of the data makes processing computationally demanding [5]. Over 70 teams from 19 countries signed up [5]. The best result came from an ensemble of YOLO and Faster R-CNN models [5], and the journal paper on the dataset gives its F1 score across all six countries as 76.9% [4]. The dataset is still in use as a benchmark, since the later Optimized Road Damage Detection Challenge (ORDDC'2024) is built on it as well [4]. Taken together, these datasets show a steady move from a single country toward multi-national data collected with diverse devices, which makes RDD2022 the broadest of the datasets reviewed here.

### 2. Object Detection and the YOLO Family

Deep learning object detectors are commonly grouped into two families. Two-stage detectors, such as R-CNN [6] and Faster R-CNN [7], first propose regions that may contain an object and then classify each region; one-stage detectors, such as SSD [8] and YOLO [9], skip the separate proposal step. R-CNN [6], short for "Regions with CNN features", runs a large CNN on region proposals produced by a bottom-up method. Faster R-CNN [7] moved the proposal step inside the network: its Region Proposal Network (RPN) works on the same convolutional features of the whole image as the detector. SSD [8] performs detection with one deep network and needs neither proposals nor a later step that resamples features.

YOLO (You Only Look Once) by Redmon et al. [9] treats detection as a single regression task: one network looks at the whole image once and outputs both the box coordinates and the class probabilities. Because each image passes through the network only once, YOLO runs in real time [9], which makes the approach suitable for the live camera mode planned in this project. Its authors also report a trade-off: compared with the leading detectors of the time, YOLO localizes objects less precisely but produces far fewer false detections on the background [9].

The three YOLO versions compared in this project were all released by Ultralytics, whose documentation [11]–[13] reports their accuracy on the COCO benchmark [10], a large general-purpose dataset of common objects. YOLOv8 uses an anchor-free split head [11]. According to Ultralytics, the medium-sized YOLO11 model reaches a higher COCO accuracy than its YOLOv8 counterpart while using fewer parameters [12]. The newest generation, YOLO26, was released in January 2026 [13]. It drops Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) to simplify the detection head and supports end-to-end inference without a separate non-maximum suppression (NMS) step [13], [14]. This is done with two heads: a one-to-one head, switched on with `nms=False`, that needs no NMS, and a default one-to-many head that still relies on NMS [13]. The accompanying paper adds a Progressive Loss that gradually moves supervision toward the head used at inference time [14]. All models in our experiments were evaluated with the default one-to-many head. YOLO26 also brings Small-Target-Aware Label Assignment (STAL) and MuSGD, an optimizer that combines SGD and Muon [13]. In short, two-stage detectors represent the earlier, slower design, while successive YOLO versions have aimed at simpler heads and faster inference, the property this project needs for its real-time camera mode.

### 3. YOLO-Based Road Damage Detection

YOLO models have played a central role in the road damage challenges. The winning GRDDC'2020 entry was an ensemble of YOLO models [3]. Doshi and Yilmaz [15] combined several YOLOv4 models trained on images from the Czech Republic, Japan, and India, and obtained F1 scores of 0.628 and 0.6358 on the two test sets. At CRDDC'2022, four of the five best teams relied only on YOLO-family models: one ensembled YOLOv5x models applied to image patches, one used YOLOv7 with augmentations, label smoothing, and coordinate attention, one built an ensemble around YOLOv5 with attention modules, and one used a YOLOv7-based ensemble [5]. Pham et al. [16], one of these teams, combined YOLOv7 with coordinate attention, label smoothing, and ensembling; with an F1 score of 74.1% on all test images, they placed third among 54 teams [16]. They also suggested Google Street View as a source for collecting and labeling road damage images [16].

Newer YOLO versions have since been adapted to the task. SEA-YOLO v8 by Zhao et al. [17] changes YOLOv8 in four places: an SBS module that reduces computational cost, an EMA attention module in the neck, an adaptive attention feature pyramid, and a lightweight shared convolutional detection head (LSCD-Head). On RDD2022 it reached 63.2% mAP50 and performed better than the original YOLOv8 [17]. The latest generation has also been applied to the problem: in a recent preprint, Youwai et al. [18] built YOLO26-RD on top of YOLO26 and reported 0.790 mAP50 averaged over five model scales, against 0.776 for the unmodified YOLO26, but on a different dataset of region-level survey imagery [18]. These results cannot be compared directly, because the studies differ in data subsets, splits, and metrics (F1 in the challenges, mAP50 in journal papers). Overall, the reviewed work shows that YOLO-based models are a strong and common choice for road damage detection, but it does not show which YOLO version performs best on the same data; this is one of the reasons why this project compares several versions under identical conditions.

### 4. Reference Project: RoadDamageDetection

The open-source RoadDamageDetection project [19] is the application closest to this study. It pairs a YOLOv8 model trained on the CRDDC'2022 dataset with a Streamlit web application that runs detection on images, videos, and live webcam input [19]. The model is a YOLOv8s, trained on an RTX 2060 GPU using only the Japan and India images, and it detects the same four damage types as this project [19]. The repository reports 0.547 mAP@0.5 over all classes; however, this value is taken from a precision-recall curve image in a GitHub repository that has not been peer reviewed, and the repository does not state on which split it was measured. It only notes that the authors created their own train and validation split because the official test labels were not available [19]. This project served as the starting point of our work and provides its reference value. Because the value comes from different images and an unspecified split, it can serve only as an approximate point of comparison.

### 5. Position of This Study

The studies above point to three gaps. First, several works use only part of the available data: the reference project covers only Japan and India [19], and the GRDDC'2020 solutions covered only India, Japan, and the Czech Republic [3], [15]. This project uses the annotated RDD2022 training data from all six countries with the four standard damage classes (D00, D10, D20, D40) of the RDD benchmarks [2], [4]; boxes of the "other corruption" class (white line blur and crosswalk blur) were left out, because they describe worn road markings rather than damage to the pavement surface. Second, published YOLO results on road damage are reported on different subsets and with different metrics, so it is hard to tell whether a newer YOLO version is actually better [3], [5], [17], [18]. Third, the comparisons summarized above are based mainly on accuracy, whereas a real-time application also needs to know how fast a model runs on the hardware it will be used on. To address these points, this project compares YOLOv8s [11], YOLO11s [12], and YOLO26s [13] on the same data split, with the same training settings and the same evaluation script, and measures their speed as well as their accuracy; the selected final model, YOLO26s with a 1024-pixel input, reaches a validation mAP50 of 0.609 and runs at about 25 FPS on a GTX 1650 GPU. Like the reference project [19], this project is developing a Streamlit web interface for image, video, and real-time camera input.

   Metinde GDJ'nin "76.9%" değeri [4] ile kullanıldı.

   Metinde 26.336 [2] kullanıldı.

   (kullanıcı IEEE sayfasında doğruladı; Semantic Scholar kaydı da 70), Sec. IV "More than 50 teams", Table V "64".

   "54 teams", IEEE özeti "54 leaders"; kaynakça IEEE sürümüne çevrildi, metinde **54** kullanıldı.

   2026-11-10'da düzeltildi (yolo-modelleri.md: "doğrulanamadı, raporda kaynak olarak kullanılmayacak" notu).

   2026-11-10'da düzeltildi (yolo-modelleri.md / model-secimi.md: "Bir topluluk sonucuna göre (Hugging Face model kartı, hakemsiz)").

- **Genel (2026-11-10 sadeleştirme):** Cümleler kaynak özetlerinden kopyalanmış gibi durmasın diye kendi cümlelerimizle
  yazıldı (benzerlik/intihal denetimi riski); anlam ve sayılar aynı. Her bölüm (§1–§3) önceki paragraflardan çıkan kısa bir
  sentez cümlesiyle bitiyor; bu cümleler yeni iddia getirmiyor, yalnızca özetliyor. Kendi bilgilerimiz "(this study)" eki
  yerine "this project" / "our experiments" ifadeleriyle kaynaklı iddialardan ayrılıyor.
- **§1 Veri setleri ve yarışmalar:** Şablon "alandaki en güncel durumu" istiyor; bu alanda güncel durumu veri setleri ve yarışmalar belirliyor.
  Japonya (tek ülke, 8 tür) → RDD2020 (3 ülke, 4 tür) → RDD2022 (6 ülke, 4 tür) çizgisi, bizim veri setimizin nereden geldiğini ve neden
  4 sınıf kullandığımızı açıklıyor. Yüksek çözünürlük cümlesi Norveç'i küçültme kararımızın literatürdeki dayanağı. Sentez: veri tek
  ülkeden çok ülkeye ve çeşitli cihazlara genişledi; RDD2022 incelenenlerin en geniş olanı.
  *Anlatım:* "Bu alan Japonya'da tek ülkelik bir veri setiyle başladı, her yarışmada ülke sayısı arttı. Biz en güncel ve en büyük olan RDD2022'yi kullanıyoruz."
- **§2 Nesne tespiti ve YOLO:** Hocanın "neden YOLO?" sorusuna cevap. İki aşamalı (R-CNN, Faster R-CNN) ile tek aşamalı (SSD, YOLO) farkı ve
  YOLO'nun hız avantajı kamera hedefimizi gerekçelendiriyor. Hız artık sayısız (nitel) anlatılıyor; 45/155 FPS, COCO değerleri ve çıkış
  tarihleri rapora gereksiz ayrıntı olduğu için çıkarıldı (sorulursa Kaynak Kontrol Tablosu'nda duruyor). v8 → 11 → 26 sürümleri karşılaştırdığımız
  üç modelin tanıtımı. Sentez: iki aşamalı eski ve yavaş tasarım, YOLO sürümleri sadeleşme ve hız yönünde ilerliyor.
  *Anlatım:* "Faster R-CNN önce aday bölge çıkarıp sonra sınıflıyor; YOLO resmi bir kerede işleyip kutuları doğrudan tahmin ediyor, bu yüzden gerçek zamanlıya uygun."
