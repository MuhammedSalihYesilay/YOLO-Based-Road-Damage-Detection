## LITERATURE ANALYSIS

### 1. Road Damage Datasets and Challenges

Research on automatic road damage detection has been shaped by a series of public datasets and open challenges. An early large-scale dataset was introduced by Maeda et al. [1], who collected 9,053 road damage images captured with a smartphone installed on a car, containing 15,435 instances of road surface damage. The images were gathered in cooperation with 7 municipalities in Japan, and the authors showed that, with a convolutional neural network-based object detector, the type of damage can be classified into eight types with high accuracy [1]. Building on this line of work, the first Road Damage Detection Challenge, RDDC'2018, was organized as an IEEE Big Data Cup in 2018 [5]. It targeted only Japan, attracted 59 teams, and its best model was an ensemble of Faster R-CNN and SSD detectors that reached a score of 0.665 [5].

The second step was RDD2020 [2], a dataset of 26,336 road images from India, Japan, and the Czech Republic with more than 31,000 instances of road damage. RDD2020 reduced the label set to four damage categories: longitudinal cracks (D00), transverse cracks (D10), alligator cracks (D20), and potholes (D40) [2]. The images were captured with vehicle-mounted smartphones and annotated in PASCAL VOC format [2]. RDD2020 formed the basis of the Global Road Damage Detection Challenge (GRDDC'2020), which was also organized as an IEEE Big Data Cup [3]. In total, 121 teams registered, and the best-performing model used YOLO-based ensemble learning to obtain an F1 score of 0.67 on the first test set and 0.66 on the second [3].

The most recent dataset in this series is RDD2022 [4]. It contains 47,420 road images from six countries (Japan, India, the Czech Republic, Norway, the United States, and China) and more than 55,000 instances of road damage, using the same four damage types: longitudinal cracks, transverse cracks, alligator cracks, and potholes [4]. RDD2022 was released as part of the Crowdsensing-based Road Damage Detection Challenge (CRDDC'2022) [4]. In CRDDC'2022, the model architecture was not predefined, so participants could propose any architecture [5]. The data were captured using multiple mechanisms, including smartphone-mounted vehicles, wide-angle cameras, drones, and motorbikes, with different image resolutions [5]. The organizers also noted that the inclusion of high-resolution images in RDD2022 led to the requirement of heavy computation resources to process the data [5]. More than 60 teams from 19 countries registered for CRDDC'2022 [5]. The best-performing model utilized ensemble learning based on YOLO and Faster R-CNN series models [5], and the journal version of the dataset paper reports an F1 score of 76.9% for road damage detection in all six countries [4]. RDD2022 continues to be used as a benchmark: the later Optimized Road Damage Detection Challenge (ORDDC'2024) is also based on it [4].

### 2. Object Detection and the YOLO Family

Deep learning-based object detectors can be divided into two-stage detectors, such as R-CNN [6] and Faster R-CNN [7], and one-stage detectors, such as SSD [8] and YOLO [9]. Two-stage detectors first generate candidate regions and then classify them. R-CNN [6] introduced this idea by applying high-capacity convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to bottom-up region proposals; its name stands for "Regions with CNN features". Faster R-CNN [7] made the proposal step part of the network through a Region Proposal Network (RPN) that shares full-image convolutional features with the detection network, and reached a frame rate of 5 fps on a GPU. One-stage detectors remove the separate proposal step. SSD [8] detects objects using a single deep neural network and completely eliminates proposal generation and the subsequent feature resampling stage.

YOLO (You Only Look Once), proposed by Redmon et al. [9], frames object detection as a regression problem to spatially separated bounding boxes and associated class probabilities. A single neural network predicts bounding boxes and class probabilities directly from full images in one evaluation. The base YOLO model processes images in real time at 45 frames per second, and a smaller version, Fast YOLO, processes 155 frames per second [9]. This speed makes the YOLO approach suitable for real-time applications such as the live camera mode planned in this study.

The three YOLO versions compared in this study were released by Ultralytics, whose documentation [11]–[13] reports their accuracy on the COCO benchmark [10]; COCO contains 2.5 million labeled instances in 328k images. YOLOv8 was released by Ultralytics on January 10, 2023 and adopts an anchor-free split head; its small model (YOLOv8s) reaches 44.9 mAP50-95 on COCO with 11.2 M parameters [11]. Ultralytics notes that no formal research paper was published for YOLOv8 [11]. YOLO11 was released on September 27, 2024; according to Ultralytics, YOLO11m achieves a higher mAP on COCO while using 22% fewer parameters than YOLOv8m, and YOLO11s reaches 47.0 mAP50-95 with 9.4 M parameters [12]. The newest generation, YOLO26, was released in January 2026 [13]. Two of its main architectural changes are the removal of Distribution Focal Loss (DFL), which reduces detection-head complexity, and native end-to-end inference without a separate non-maximum suppression (NMS) step [13], [14]. YOLO26 uses a dual-head design: the one-to-one head (enabled with `nms=False`) produces predictions without NMS, while the default one-to-many head still uses NMS [13]. It also introduces Small-Target-Aware Label Assignment (STAL) and the MuSGD optimizer, a hybrid of SGD and Muon [13]. Ultralytics reports up to 43% faster CPU ONNX inference for YOLO26n compared with YOLO11n, and YOLO26s reaches 48.6 mAP50-95 on COCO with 9.5 M parameters [13].

### 3. YOLO-Based Road Damage Detection

YOLO models have been central to the road damage detection challenges. In GRDDC'2020, the winning solution was a YOLO-based ensemble [3]. Doshi and Yilmaz [15] trained an ensemble of YOLOv4 models on road damage images from the Czech Republic, Japan, and India, and reached F1 scores of 0.628 and 0.6358 on the two test sets. In CRDDC'2022, four of the five best teams used only YOLO-family models: an ensemble of YOLOv5x models with image patches, YOLOv7 with augmentations, label smoothing and coordinate attention, an ensemble based on YOLOv5 and attention modules, and a YOLOv7-based ensemble [5]. One of these teams, Pham et al. [16], used YOLOv7 with coordinate attention, label smoothing, and ensembling, and obtained an F1 score of 74.1% on all test images, ranking third among 51 teams.

Recent studies have adapted newer YOLO versions to road damage detection. Zhao et al. [17] proposed SEA-YOLO v8, a modified YOLOv8 model that achieved 63.2% mAP50 on RDD2022 and performed better than the original YOLOv8 model. The newest generation is also being applied to the problem: in a recent preprint, Youwai et al. [18] proposed YOLO26-RD, a road damage detector based on YOLO26, which reached 0.790 mAP50 averaged over model scales, compared with 0.776 for the unmodified YOLO26, on a different dataset (region-level survey imagery) [18]. Because these studies use different subsets, data splits, and metrics (F1 in the challenges, mAP50 in journal papers), their numbers cannot be compared directly. This is one of the reasons why this study compares several YOLO versions under identical conditions.

### 4. Reference Project: RoadDamageDetection

A closely related application is the open-source RoadDamageDetection project [19], which combines a YOLOv8 model trained on the CRDDC'2022 dataset with a Streamlit web application for detection on images, videos, and real-time (webcam) input. The model is a YOLOv8s trained on a filtered subset containing only the Japan and India images, using an RTX 2060 GPU [19]. It detects the same four damage types as this study: longitudinal cracks, transverse cracks, alligator cracks, and potholes [19]. Its published training results include a precision-recall curve reporting 0.547 mAP@0.5 over all classes; the project created its own train and validation split because the original test labels were not available [19]. This project was used as the starting point and the reference value for this study. Its value cannot be compared exactly with ours, because it was measured on a different set of images.

### 5. Position of This Study

The studies above show three gaps that this project addresses. First, several works cover only part of the available countries: the reference project uses only Japan and India [19], and the GRDDC'2020 solutions covered only India, Japan, and the Czech Republic [3], [15]. This study uses the four standard damage classes (D00, D10, D20, D40) [2], [4]. The annotated training portion of RDD2022 (38,385 images from all six countries) was split into training, validation, and test sets; background-only images were then reduced to about 10% of the training set only, leaving 28,887 images (21,211 / 3,838 / 3,838) (this study). Boxes of the "other corruption" class (blurred road markings) were excluded, because they describe paint wear rather than pavement damage (this study). Second, published YOLO results on road damage are reported on different subsets and with different metrics, which makes it hard to tell whether a newer YOLO version is actually better [3], [5], [17], [18]. This study trains YOLOv8s [11], YOLO11s [12], and YOLO26s [13] on the same data split, with the same training settings and the same evaluation script, so that only the model changes. In this comparison, YOLO26s reached the highest validation mAP50 (0.589), ahead of YOLO11s (0.582) and YOLOv8s (0.571) (this study). Third, the road damage studies reviewed above report detection accuracy; this study additionally measures inference speed on its own target hardware, against a real-time target of at least 15 FPS on a GTX 1650 GPU (this study). Like the reference project [19], this study is developing a Streamlit web interface for image, video, and real-time camera input.
